3.3.4 Feature Importance and Hyperparameter Ablation

Understanding the decision-making process of the XGBoost PCG classifier is critical for clinical trust. Feature importance analysis, derived from the trained XGBoost model's internal node splitting criteria, reveals the relative contribution of the 1,224 dimensions. The analysis demonstrates a strong synergy between the traditional acoustic features and the deep YAMNet embeddings. Among the traditional features, the Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs)—specifically the lower-order coefficients that capture the broad spectral envelope of the heart sounds—ranked consistently high. Spectral contrast, which measures the difference in amplitude between peaks and valleys in the frequency spectrum, also proved highly discriminative for identifying the harsh, broad-spectrum noise characteristic of systolic murmurs.

However, the YAMNet embeddings contributed heavily to the model's robustness against background noise. YAMNet, trained on the massive AudioSet corpus to recognize over 500 audio classes, inherently learned to separate foreground acoustic events from stationary background noise. This capability proved invaluable when processing the CirCor dataset, which contains significant ambient clinical noise. The combination of targeted, hand-crafted cardiac features and general-purpose robust embeddings is the primary driver behind the model's 83.65% accuracy.

To optimize the XGBoost classifier, the AI team conducted a rigorous hyperparameter ablation study. The maximum tree depth (max_depth) was evaluated at values of 6, 8, and 10. A depth of 6 resulted in underfitting (low training and validation accuracy), while a depth of 10 caused severe overfitting (near 99% training accuracy but degraded validation accuracy). A max_depth of 8 provided the optimal balance. The learning rate (eta) was tested at 0.01, 0.03, and 0.1. The lower rate of 0.03, combined with a higher number of boosting rounds (700 trees), allowed the model to converge smoothly without oscillating around the local minima, outperforming the aggressive 0.1 rate. Finally, the subsample ratio was set to 0.85, meaning each tree is trained on a random 85% subset of the data, which acts as a powerful regularizer against overfitting to specific patients in the training fold.

3.3.5 Per-Class Performance and Clinical Thresholds

While overall accuracy provides a general measure of model performance, clinical applications require a nuanced understanding of how the model performs on specific diagnostic classes. The evaluation of the XGBoost PCG model on the pristine 20% test set yielded the following per-class metrics:

- Normal Class: Precision 84.1%, Recall 81.6%, F1-Score 82.8%
- Murmur Class: Precision 81.2%, Recall 82.3%, F1-Score 81.7%
- Artifact Class: Precision 88.5%, Recall 90.1%, F1-Score 89.3%

The slight bias toward higher recall for the Murmur class (82.3%) compared to its precision (81.2%) reflects a deliberate clinical thresholding strategy. In a screening context, a false positive (flagging a normal heart sound as a potential murmur) is preferable to a false negative (missing a true murmur), as false positives will be subsequently reviewed and dismissed by a human clinician, whereas a false negative may result in a missed diagnosis of structural heart disease. The high performance on the Artifact class demonstrates the pipeline's effectiveness at identifying and isolating recordings that are too degraded by motion or contact noise for reliable diagnosis.

3.3.8 Model Deployment and Version Management

The integration of the trained AI models into the live FastAPI inference service required a robust version management and deployment strategy. The AI team implemented the `model_registry.py` module to handle the lifecycle of all predictive models. Models are exported from their respective training environments in standard serialization formats (scikit-learn Pickles for XGBoost, HDF5/Keras formats for neural networks) and stored in the `new-models/` directory.

The model registry uses a lazy-loading singleton pattern. Upon the first inference request, the required models are loaded into memory and cached, minimizing latency for subsequent requests. The registry enforces a strict naming convention, such as `AuscultICor_v26_SL.keras`, which embeds the model architecture, version number, and lead configuration directly in the filename. 

Crucially, the FastAPI service supports a hot-reload capability. When the AI team wishes to deploy an improved model, they simply upload the new model file to the server and update the configuration pointer via an authenticated API endpoint. The model registry safely evicts the old model from memory and loads the new version without requiring a restart of the Docker container, ensuring zero downtime for the inference service. In the event of anomalous predictions, the DevOps team can execute an instantaneous rollback to the previous version using the same mechanism.
